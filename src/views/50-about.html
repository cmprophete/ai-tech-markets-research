Welcome to our AI dashboard.

The AI transition is one of the most challenging transformations of our times. We don’t know what this technology will become, or what policy should do in response.

Each question calls for a different kind of answer, which is how this dashboard is organized. We’re learning here alongside everyone else, but we think what we’ve built can contribute to a better AI transition.

Research

It’s unclear how the technology itself will take shape. Will it be a normal technology, as Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue, or is it more like electricity, where the whole economy eventually reorganizes around it?

Our research tracker is a searchable, tagged list of work on AI and its impact, broadly defined. Alongside it is our fact bank, meant as a resource for the community as a whole: specific sentences you can consider using, each with a caveat section laying out what the finding actually supports and where it has been stretched.

How this section is built. We choose the research by hand and aim to add to it every month. To cover this much ground, Claude pulls the facts and drafts the summaries, and a person reviews them: each fact says which model extracted it, and each summary says whether it has been reviewed yet. We’ve built skills to check the output for accuracy, but as with any secondhand source, verify anything you plan to cite or build on. Terminal-based AI tools are becoming more important for ideas-based work, and we’d rather experiment with putting them into our own workflow than only write about them.

Policy

And even then, it’s hard to know how to react. What policies safeguard against the downsides, amplify what’s good, and make sure this broadly helps people? Should we work through the policies we already have, or does a genuinely new era demand bigger and different ideas? How much can we steer the technology, and how much should we just plan on absorbing its effects?

Our policy map includes nearly every idea people have raised for making AI safe and broadly beneficial. Click through any of them and you’ll find the major bills and initiatives from that policy’s advocates. If you’re trying to compile what has actually been introduced, start here.

This is also where we build on our theory of provoking and legitimizing policy solutions. In a world where AI is fundamentally disruptive, the work is provocation: ideas that go well beyond shoring up what we already have. In a world where AI mostly accelerates the prosperity and the inequality of the last several decades, the work is legitimation, and that’s the harder job. We already know what those solutions look like. They have been contested for thirty years.

Our own policy agenda is coming soon.

Government action

Meanwhile, governments are already acting. What are Congress and the states actually putting on the table, and where does each bill stand? What has been enforced under the laws we already have, and what have the courts said?

Our legislation tracker lists the bills about AI that are active in Congress and the states, alongside the results of the 2026 midterms. Point at a state to see what is happening there, from where its bills stand, introduced to enacted, to the cases and enforcement actions listed for it, then follow the links in its card to the full lists, which you can search and filter by topic (and, for bills, by status). Landscape gathers the bills behind each policy idea; this part tracks the bills themselves, wherever they are in the process. Enforcement lists what agencies and attorneys general have done about AI, and Cases the court rulings that bear on it, each described in our own words and linked to the agency’s or court’s own page. All shows everything on one page: the midterm results, the bills, the cases and the enforcement actions. Each of the other three shows its own alone.

How this section is built. Federal bills come from our own search of GovInfo’s bill-status data, the official record of every bill: one is listed when its title names AI or a close relative of it, such as deepfakes or chatbots. State bills come from our own search of Open States, which follows each legislature’s own record. Summaries are the legislature’s own for state bills, and the Congressional Research Service’s for federal bills where it has written one. Midterm results come from the Associated Press, copied by hand. Enforcement and case entries are chosen by hand and written in our own words from the agency’s or court’s own page, which each one links to, and the lists are not complete. We aim to refresh the bills every week and to update the enforcement actions and cases every month. Topics are our reading of each bill’s title and summary, so check a bill’s official record before you cite it.
