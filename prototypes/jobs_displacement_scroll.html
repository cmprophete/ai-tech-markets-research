Job displacement tracker

Is AI displacing workers yet?

The headline unemployment rate is a blunt instrument. To see displacement you have to look past it: at how the weakness is distributed across workers, and at which sectors have stopped hiring. The answer changes as you go, and each section says what it can and cannot establish.

01 The level

Is unemployment higher than growth alone would predict, and is a low rate hiding people who have stopped looking?

Okun’s law: GDP growth vs. change in unemployment

Okun’s law ties the change in unemployment to how fast the economy grows: strong GDP growth pulls unemployment down, weak growth pushes it up. If unemployment were rising faster than growth predicts, that would point to something structural — a displacement effect layered on the ordinary business cycle. Each point is a quarter; the line is the historical relationship, and the last four quarters are traced in sequence.

They sit close to it. Unemployment can also mislead if people stop looking for work, since they leave the labor force and the rate falls even as the market weakens. The companion check is prime-age participation, which strips out retirement.

Prime-age (25–54) labor force participation & employment rate
Monthly, seasonally adjusted, since 2000

What this cannot establish

This is the coarsest measure on the page and the last place displacement would appear. The Okun relationship leaves most quarter-to-quarter variation unexplained, so only a very large shock would clear the noise, and it is fitted on eighty years in which nothing resembling AI displacement occurs. An effect concentrated in one cohort or one sector — which is what the next two sections report — would leave no trace here at all. Read this as ruling out a macroeconomic event, not as evidence of calm.

02 Across workers

Concentrated — young graduates carry more unemployment than their own pre-2020 norm predicts.

Is the weakness spread evenly across workers, or is one group absorbing it?

Excess unemployment by age, relative to the pre-2020 norm
Residual: actual minus predicted unemployment rate, by education group

A healthy average can hide a group taking the hit. Each age-and-education group has a predicted unemployment rate given the overall rate, estimated from its own pre-2020 relationship. The chart plots how far above or below that prediction each group actually sits. Zero is normal; positive means more unemployment than history says the group should have.

The shape is the finding. The residual is steeply positive at the youngest ages among degree-holders and flattens to roughly nothing by the mid-forties. That is not what a broad-based downturn looks like — a general slowdown pushes the whole curve up, not one end of it.

What this cannot establish

Concentration, not cause. The same curve would appear if firms had cut graduate training programs for ordinary cost reasons, or if degree supply had outrun demand in specific fields. The entry-level market was also weakening before generative AI was deployed at scale, and this residual cannot separate the two. Postings data by occupational exposure is what would distinguish them, and it is not in this tracker yet.

How this is measured
Data
Current Population Survey microdata, unemployment rates by single year of age crossed with education group.
Sample
Each group’s relationship to the overall unemployment rate is estimated on pre-2020 data only, then used to predict the current period out of sample.
Plotted
The residual in percentage points: actual unemployment rate minus the rate predicted by that group’s own historical relationship to the aggregate. Zero means the group is exactly where history says it should be.
Main caveat
Single-year age bands have small samples and are noisy; the curve, not any individual point, is the result. Residuals measure divergence from a historical norm, never its cause.
Full specification and diagnostics ↗
Would change this The young-graduate residual falling back below 1 pp, or a matched postings series showing the same pattern in occupations with no plausible AI exposure.
03 Across sectors

Where has hiring slowed the most, and is it where AI substitution is most plausible?

Job-growth slowdown by sector

Displacement should show up as hiring drying up in specific industries. For each major sector, employment growth over the past year is compared to that sector’s own average pace in 2015–2019. A large negative bar means the sector is adding jobs far more slowly than it used to, or shedding them outright. Using each sector as its own baseline avoids penalising industries that were always slow-growing.

What this cannot establish

The sector that moved is also the sector that over-hired hardest in 2021–22 and has been correcting since. That is a complete alternative explanation, and the timing does not separate it from an AI effect. Eleven supersectors is a coarse grid besides: displacement inside legal services, or inside customer support within retail, would be averaged away entirely. Occupational mix within industries is the test that would catch that.

How this is measured
Data
BLS Current Employment Statistics, all-employee payrolls for the eleven major supersectors, which sum to total nonfarm.
Sample
Plotted
Past-year employment growth minus that sector’s 2015–19 average growth, in percentage points. Negative means slower than its own pre-pandemic pace.
Main caveat
Supersector aggregates mask occupational composition, and payroll counts do not distinguish a role eliminated from a role never opened.
Full specification and diagnostics ↗
Would change this Information-sector growth returning to within 1 pp of its baseline, or the slowdown spreading to sectors with no plausible AI exposure.
