Welcome to our AI dashboard.

The AI transition is one of the most challenging transformations of our times. We don’t know what this technology will become, or what policy should do in response.

Each question calls for a different kind of answer, which is how this dashboard is organized. We’re learning here alongside everyone else, but we think what we’ve built can contribute to a better AI transition.

Research

It’s unclear how the technology itself will take shape. Will it be a normal technology, as Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue, or is it more like electricity, where the whole economy eventually reorganizes around it?

Our research tracker is a searchable, tagged list of work on AI and its impact, broadly defined. Alongside it is our fact bank, meant as a resource for the community as a whole: specific sentences you can consider using, each with a caveat section laying out what the finding actually supports and where it has been stretched.

How this section is built. To cover this much ground, the tracker and fact bank are summarized with the help of AI and then human review. We’ve built skills to check the output for accuracy, but as with any secondhand source, verify anything you plan to cite or build on. Terminal-based AI tools are becoming more important for ideas-based work, and we’d rather experiment with putting them into our own workflow than only write about them.

Policy

And even then, it’s hard to know how to react. What policies safeguard against the downsides, amplify what’s good, and make sure this broadly helps people? Should we work through the policies we already have, or does a genuinely new era demand bigger and different ideas? How much can we steer the technology, and how much should we just plan on absorbing its effects?

Our policy map includes nearly every idea people have raised for making AI safe and broadly beneficial. Click through any of them and you’ll find the major bills and initiatives from that policy’s advocates. If you’re trying to compile what has actually been introduced, start here.

This is also where we build on our theory of provoking and legitimizing policy solutions. In a world where AI is fundamentally disruptive, the work is provocation: ideas that go well beyond shoring up what we already have. In a world where AI mostly accelerates the prosperity and the inequality of the last several decades, the work is legitimation, and that’s the harder job. We already know what those solutions look like. They have been contested for thirty years.

Government action

Meanwhile, governments are already acting. What are Congress and the states actually putting on the table, and where does each bill stand? What has been enforced under the laws we already have, and what have the courts said?

Our legislation tracker lists the bills about AI that are active in Congress and the states, alongside the results of the 2026 midterms. Point at a state to see how its bills stand, from introduced to enacted, then follow the link to the full list, which you can search and filter by status and topic. Landscape gathers the bills behind each policy idea; this part tracks the bills themselves, wherever they are in the process. Enforcement, for agency actions and key cases, is still to come.

How this section is built. Federal bills come from the Brennan Center’s AI legislation tracker, with each bill’s official record read from GovInfo. State bills come from our own search of Open States, which follows each legislature’s own record; where it has no summary for a state, we show the legislature’s own. Midterm results come from the Associated Press. Topics are our reading of each bill’s title and summary, and the federal summaries are in our own words, written with the help of AI, so check a bill’s official record before you cite it.
