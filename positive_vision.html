What should we demand of AI?

AI should widen who gets to know and do things. That is a good place to start. The ambition is to expand people’s practical capabilities: what they can understand, create, decide, and accomplish, and the lives they can choose to lead.

These demands belong at the door of the companies building AI, the employers and agencies deploying it, and the governments setting its rules. People have standing to shape those choices, set conditions, and refuse harmful uses. Benefits should be demonstrated, and promises should come with obligations.

These are notes for an argument we are still developing, not an adopted policy platform. The goals below can conflict. Naming those conflicts is part of the project.

What this project should do

Give people a way to judge AI’s development and a concrete picture of what to demand from it. “Shared prosperity” should mean something specific enough to distinguish a good technological transition from a bad one.

The project should describe a future worth building, explain the incentives and conflicts that could prevent it, and show how public action could bring it closer. Research and data should help us test those arguments, including when the evidence challenges a policy we favor.

What would have to be true for AI to make ordinary people more capable, more secure, and more powerful? How would we know it was happening?

Three parts of the ambition

Technology

Broaden access to expertise and the ability to build.

Start with what we want people to become able to do: understand a difficult problem, deliver better care, navigate public services, build a useful tool, or exercise judgment that previously required resources they could not access.

Ask which needs attract investment, who can develop alternatives, and who controls access and sets the terms. Public research, public capacity, competition, and procurement are possible ways to shape that direction. Privacy, safety, and the right to refuse harmful uses are conditions of useful access.

The test: Whose capabilities expand? Are those capabilities reliable, affordable, and under terms people can influence?

Work

Turn greater productive capacity into better working lives.

A faster task is only the beginning of the story. We should ask whether AI expands workers’ judgment, creates opportunities to learn, and opens routes into skilled work. We should also ask when it removes discretion, intensifies work, or undermines the path by which people acquire expertise.

Trace any gains through wages, working time, prices, service quality, profits, and new work. Bargaining rights and worker participation can shape that distribution; demand, competition, and the organization of production also matter. A saved hour does not come with a predetermined beneficiary.

The test: Does greater productive capacity improve people’s livelihoods and choices? Who has the power to negotiate the terms?

Economic security

Give people security and freedom through economic change.

People should be able to withstand displacement, leave a bad employer, retrain, care for someone, or try something new without risking destitution. Support must arrive when it is needed and reach people whose working lives do not fit a tidy employment record.

Income protection, healthcare, and accessible benefits can provide that security. Sharing the returns from greater social wealth raises an additional question: how should prosperity reach people outside paid employment? Social insurance and a claim on shared wealth serve related but distinct purposes.

The test: Do people have meaningful options through change? Does greater prosperity strengthen security beyond those who hold a job or own capital?

What connects them

The connecting idea is effective freedom: the ability to do something useful, the power to influence the conditions under which you do it, and the security to make meaningful choices.

Access to expertise is precarious if a platform can withdraw it or dictate its terms. Productivity gains may leave workers with little benefit if they have no bargaining power. Income protection can give people room to refuse bad work, but it cannot by itself give them control over workplace decisions.

These connections are propositions to investigate. We should specify the institutions that make them work and measure the outcomes, rather than assume that putting three desirable policies together makes them mutually reinforcing.

The goals can conflict

There is no guarantee that expanded access, better jobs, security, and safety move together. Any agenda for governing AI has to face difficult choices.

  • Access and incumbent earnings. Making expertise widely available can weaken the earnings or bargaining position of people who previously supplied it. We need to distinguish protecting livelihoods from preserving every existing restriction on access.
  • Job preservation and better provision. Keeping an existing task or staffing arrangement may conflict with cheaper or more accessible services. The question is what changes, who benefits, and what obligations accompany the change.
  • Efficiency today and expertise tomorrow. Automating junior tasks may save time while removing opportunities to learn. A deployment should be judged partly by whether it sustains the development of future expertise.
  • Broad access and protection from harm. Making powerful tools easier to obtain can also make harmful uses easier. Access rules need to reflect the capabilities and uses at issue.
  • Income security and concentrated control. Transfers can protect living standards while ownership and decision-making remain concentrated. Sharing income does not settle who governs the technology.

The task is to make these tensions visible, identify who bears the costs, and explain why a particular institutional choice is justified. “Everyone wins” cannot be the premise.

Make the vision answerable to evidence

Each story should connect an observed result, a contested explanation, and a decision someone can make. Distinguish measured outcomes from illustrative scenarios and aspirations. Technical exposure is not adoption; task-level speed is not economy-wide productivity; productivity is not automatically higher wages or greater security.

For each proposal, name the actor, the instrument, the intended outcome, and what would count as failure. Evidence should help us decide when to encourage adoption, change its terms, or stop a use.

Intellectual starting points include Building pro-worker AI and ESP’s Ideas for Shared Economic Prosperity in the AI Transition. This page proposes a framing for further work; it does not establish empirical effects for the policies discussed.

Questions to keep working on

  1. Which expansion of human capability should anchor the first story, and whose life would it change?
  2. Where do private incentives most clearly diverge from that ambition?
  3. Which conflict are we willing to take a position on, and what evidence would change our view?
  4. What should a reader be able to decide after using this project that they cannot decide now?

Make technological progress expand what people can do, improve the terms on which they work, and strengthen their freedom to choose.

The Problem to Solve / Mike Konczal · Working argument

We need to know what we are trying to accomplish.

Too much of the AI policy debate leaves me with a list of procedures and too little sense of where we want to land. It is very lawyerly and very administrative. It can specify an obligation without making clear what kind of technological society that obligation is meant to help build.

That is my criticism of the debate’s emphasis, not a claim that every agenda lacks substantive goals. Legal rights, administrative competence, and enforceable rules are indispensable. But they need an account of what people should become able to do, how work should improve, and what security everyone should be able to count on.

1. We are trying to hit a target that has not appeared yet.

We do not know how powerful AI will become, how quickly it will diffuse, or how extensively employers will reorganize production around it. Technical capability, adoption, productivity, and employment are different things. A forecast about one does not settle the others.

Policy can become an attempt to aim regulation at an imagined endpoint: mass displacement, modest assistance, extraordinary abundance, or catastrophic loss of control. Choose a different forecast and the agenda changes. Meanwhile, people need decisions about systems being deployed now.

We should distinguish uncertainty about the technology from uncertainty about our purposes. We can demand greater autonomy at work, protection against surveillance, democratic authority over dangerous systems, and a guarantee of economic security without knowing the eventual employment effect of AI.

This does not justify waiting. Existing harms warrant action on the evidence available; potentially irreversible harms can require action before their full extent is known. The task is to establish durable goals, specify the evidence that should trigger different interventions, and build the capacity to revise policy as conditions change.

We cannot specify the future technology in advance. We can specify what people should be entitled to demand of it.

2. The technology will outlast today’s corporate lineup.

Our instinct is to regulate the companies in front of us. That is necessary: firms make investment decisions, control infrastructure, set contracts, and exercise market power. But a durable agenda also has to govern technologies and their uses across changes in ownership, business models, and market structure.

Whether today’s valuations prove justified or collapse is a different question from whether existing capabilities remain available. A company can fail while its software, model weights, expertise, or assets pass to others. Older models can remain useful after a more capable model appears. Widely distributed weights cannot reliably be recalled merely because their original developer changes course.

Models developed in China and elsewhere belong in this account too. A domestic firm’s market position does not determine whether a capability can be deployed locally, adapted by another developer, or incorporated into a different product. That does not make every model equally capable or every service permanent. It means our framework cannot depend on the survival or dominance of a particular American company.

Regulating a technology still means assigning obligations to people and institutions: developers, distributors, infrastructure providers, employers, public agencies, and other deployers. The relevant obligation should follow the capability, use, risk, or power at issue, with enforcement responsibilities made explicit.

A workplace surveillance rule should remain meaningful when an employer changes vendors or runs an older model itself. Requirements for a consequential public decision should survive a supplier’s acquisition or bankruptcy. Controls on dangerous capabilities need to consider what happens after distribution, alongside the choices available before release.

Competition policy remains essential where firms control access or capture rents. It cannot carry the whole burden of governing what AI does. A competitive market can still supply intrusive surveillance; a public system can still violate rights.

3. Administrative machinery needs a destination.

An assessment, disclosure, audit, or review process can be useful. Its value depends on what it reveals, who can act on it, and what changes as a result. Completing the procedure is not evidence that work became better, that people gained power, or that a dangerous deployment was prevented.

My frustration is that the debate too often begins with the instrument. I want it to begin with the outcome and the conflict: whose capabilities should expand, whose authority needs limits, who should receive the gains, and which risks no private actor should be free to impose.

“AI should widen who gets to know and do things” is a starting standard. It gives us a reason to build public capacity, a reason to contest domination at work, and a reason to distribute prosperity beyond employment. It also requires limits where one actor’s expanded capabilities undermine everyone else’s freedom or safety.

The aim is an agenda that can explain both what we want to make possible and what we are prepared to stop.

Suggestions for developing this critique

4. Other reasons the debate may feel inadequate

These are possible additions to Mike’s argument, offered for discussion rather than presented as settled judgments about the field.

  1. It assigns people too passive a role. People appear as workers to retrain, consumers to inform, or victims to compensate. What would change if they were treated as builders, professionals, owners, and citizens with authority over deployment?
  2. It can mistake compliance for a transfer of power. A firm may complete every required procedure while retaining unilateral control. Ask which decisions workers, residents, or public institutions gain the power to change.
  3. It separates questions that determine each other. Technology design, workplace organization, and distribution often appear in different policy conversations. Our contribution could be to follow one deployment through all three and show where intervention changes the outcome.
  4. It has too little account of public capacity. Restricting harmful uses is essential. We also need institutions capable of evaluating systems, purchasing well, developing alternatives, and supplying services. A right that nobody can enforce and a public option nobody can operate both fall short.
  5. It leaves too little room for evidence to change the prescription. An agenda should say what would lead us to encourage a use, renegotiate its terms, restrict it, or abandon it. Otherwise research becomes decoration for positions already chosen.
  6. It struggles to describe an ordinary life made better. People should be able to picture the result: more discretion at work, time to care, accessible expertise, less surveillance, and security through change. Those outcomes give the administrative details political meaning.

Scope and grounding: this is an editorial diagnosis, not a systematic survey of policy agendas. The OECD’s anticipatory governance framework explicitly connects values, foresight, participation, and adaptive regulation, an important counterexample to an overly broad critique. NTIA’s discussion of widely available model weights addresses the difficulty of reversing release and monitoring subsequent use. The implications drawn here for a durable policy agenda are our argument, not forecasts of company failures or model performance.

Technology / Capability and control

Greater capability should come with greater public authority.

AI should widen who gets to know and do things. That includes protecting people’s ability to live without intrusive surveillance, govern themselves, and have a say over risks they cannot individually escape.

Expanding one actor’s capabilities can diminish other people’s freedom or safety. An employer may gain the ability to monitor every movement. A political operator may gain the ability to impersonate trusted voices at scale. A developer may gain capabilities whose risks fall on people who never agreed to use its product.

Our demand is to expand useful capabilities while establishing enforceable limits on the power to impose harm. Safety, democratic authority, and access belong in the same argument.

What should AI enable?

More people should be able to access expertise, solve problems, build useful tools, and improve public services. Public research, public computing capacity, competition, and procurement can help shape what gets built and who can use it.

The test is practical: whose capabilities expand, on what terms, and with what evidence of benefit? A new tool must earn its place alongside alternatives such as more staff, simpler rules, or better conventional software.

What must AI preserve?

Surveillance

The freedom to live and work without being constantly watched.

Being able to observe, infer, and score more of someone’s life does not establish a right to do so. The question is what employers, governments, and platforms should be allowed to collect and infer, for which purposes, and with what consequences.

The demand: Enforceable limits on collection, inference, retention, and use; meaningful worker and public authority over deployment; and ways to challenge decisions made from surveillance data. Consent alone is a weak foundation where refusing could cost someone their job or access to an essential service.

The conflict: Monitoring may serve a specific safety or service purpose while also enabling intrusive control. Require a justified purpose, evidence of necessity, and consideration of less intrusive alternatives. Some uses should be off limits.

Election interference

The ability to govern ourselves.

Wider access to communication tools can coexist with impersonation, deception, and manipulation. People need to be able to participate in political life without losing the ability to identify who is trying to influence them or distinguish authentic election instructions from fraudulent ones.

The demand: Accountability for deceptive electoral conduct, transparency about the actors behind political campaigns, and institutions able to investigate and respond to interference. Measures should address the conduct and its consequences, including when AI increases its reach.

The conflict: Protecting elections must also protect political expression, satire, and dissent. “Protect democracy” cannot become an unlimited authority to control speech. Restrictions need clear boundaries, independent scrutiny, and avenues for challenge.

Catastrophic and existential risk

No private actor should decide alone what danger everyone must bear.

Some possible harms extend far beyond the people choosing to build or use a system. Catastrophic risks deserve their own assessment; existential risks raise the further prospect of irreversible loss of humanity’s future. Neither is adequately addressed by an individual user’s consent or a remedy after the damage.

The demand: Developers should have to demonstrate that systems meet publicly established safety requirements before release where capabilities warrant them. Independent evaluation capacity, enforceable release conditions, continued monitoring, and authority to restrict or stop deployment should support that obligation.

The conflict: Risk estimates and evaluation methods can be uncertain or contested. The severity of a possible harm does not establish its probability; uncertainty does not establish safety either. Requirements must state what evidence is needed, who judges it, and how decisions can be reviewed as knowledge changes. Compliance must not become a privilege only incumbent firms can afford.

Who can make these demands stick?

Rights need institutions with the expertise, resources, independence, and authority to enforce them. Public agencies must be able to investigate and intervene. Workers and affected communities need standing to challenge deployments. Public buyers need the capacity to evaluate what they purchase and exit arrangements that fail.

A more competitive market does not by itself protect people from surveillance. Public ownership does not by itself establish accountability. A safety evaluation does not settle whether a particular workplace use is acceptable. Different powers address different problems.

Whose capabilities expand? Whose freedom becomes vulnerable? Who can set the terms, and who can say no?

These are proposed principles and institutional directions, not descriptions of current legal requirements or estimates of risk. Further work should connect each demand to evidence, a specific instrument, and a test of whether it works.

Work / How should we organize production?

More time. Richer work. Greater power.

Greater productive capacity should improve the terms on which people work. We should demand jobs with more judgment, less drudgery, shorter hours, and a collective say over change.

This chapter concerns how we produce things: the tasks people perform, how they learn, how work is managed, and who makes decisions. These are demands for an AI-shaped economy, not predictions of what technology will deliver.

Better livelihoods connect this chapter to economic security. Pay and working time are negotiated inside production, while taxes, public services, ownership, and social insurance also determine how people live. The distinction helps organize the questions; it does not separate the mechanisms.

What should better work mean?

Time

Shorter hours, with pay protected and room to breathe.

Some productivity gains should return to workers as time. A shorter day should not require cramming eight hours of effort into six. Predictable schedules, adequate staffing, breaks, and control over when work happens belong alongside a shorter working week.

The question: Does the redesign reduce the burden of work, or merely increase its intensity? What arrangements protect pay and service coverage?

Expertise

More people able to do skilled, consequential work.

AI should broaden access to expertise and give workers more scope to exercise judgment. People should have opportunities to learn, take responsibility, and enter occupations previously out of reach.

Preserve the routes through which expertise develops. Removing junior tasks can also remove opportunities to practice. Paid learning time, mentoring, and deliberate pathways into skilled work should be part of deployment.

The question: Who becomes more capable, and who loses the chance to become capable?

Care and education

More human attention where it matters.

Use tools to reduce burdens that crowd out care, teaching, and relationships. Judge the result by the quality of service and working life, including whether staff gain time to listen, explain, and respond to individual needs.

More care and education may require more human labor in those sectors even while total working time falls. Making that possible requires resources and staffing decisions beyond any individual workplace.

The question: Does the time saved become better provision, or disappear into staffing cuts and higher workloads?

Autonomy

Less domination at work.

“Fewer bosses” names a real aspiration: less arbitrary authority, intrusive supervision, and permission-seeking. Simply removing managers could leave workers answering to an even more rigid algorithm. The demand is greater discretion over methods, pace, and professional judgment.

Collective bargaining, worker ownership, and democratic forms of management offer different ways to change who holds authority. Each needs explicit decision rights and accountability.

The question: Can workers challenge a target, override a bad recommendation, or refuse an unsafe instruction without retaliation?

Drudgery and danger

Less exhausting, hazardous, and degrading work.

Workers should help identify the burdens worth removing. A task that looks repetitive from outside may carry judgment, social connection, or an opportunity to learn. A useful redesign should reduce physical and mental strain without transferring it to someone less visible.

The question: Whose burden falls, whose rises, and what happens to the people whose tasks disappear?

Collective power

A say before the workflow hardens.

Workers should help decide what gets automated, what stays human, and how gains are used. Advance notice, access to relevant information, bargaining rights, and enforceable agreements can give participation practical force.

The question: Which decisions can workers actually change? Consultation without authority does not establish control.

The conflicts we need to face

Wider access to expertise may unsettle occupational hierarchies and incumbent earnings. Preserving every existing task may restrict useful changes in service delivery. Shorter hours at unchanged pay require a way to cover costs and sustain provision; productivity gains will differ across workplaces.

Protecting people does not require preserving every job design. It does require a credible account of who benefits, who bears the disruption, and what power and security workers have through the change.

Could we show the same technology producing two different working lives, and identify the institutions that make the difference?

Working policy ambitions, not estimated effects. Future cases should track pay, actual hours, work intensity, autonomy, learning, staffing, and service quality together.

Economic security / How should we live?

Greater prosperity should give everyone more freedom to choose how they live.

Economic security should be a guarantee. People should have the resources to live well, withstand change, and make meaningful choices, including when they are outside paid employment.

The promise of greater productive capacity is a better life: time to care for someone, the ability to leave a bad job, access to healthcare, a stable home, and room to try something new. Whether AI contributes to that promise depends on how income, wealth, services, and economic power are distributed.

Social insurance supplies a dependable foundation. Shared wealth and public services expand what becomes possible. These are demands on the institutions governing the economy, not benefits we can assume will follow from technological progress.

Four demands for economic security

A guarantee of basic security.

Everyone should be able to count on the essentials of a decent life through unemployment, illness, disability, caregiving, and changes in work. Income protection, healthcare, and access to essential services should be dependable commitments, backed by the funding and institutions needed to deliver them.

A guarantee has to work in time. Support that arrives after someone loses their home or interrupts medical treatment has failed an essential test. Adequate benefits, accessible enrollment, continuity of coverage, and effective appeals are part of the commitment.

Security should not depend on proving that AI caused a job loss. Universal provision, earnings replacement, and additional support for particular needs can serve different purposes within the same guarantee.

The test: Can people rely on support when their circumstances change, and does it actually cover what they need?

The material freedom to choose.

Security should make it possible to leave a bad employer, learn, provide care, or try something new. People need credible alternatives before they can meaningfully choose the terms of their working lives.

That requires attention to what happens between jobs and across life stages. Losing employment should not also mean losing healthcare. Taking time to care for a family member should not require financial ruin. Returning to education should be an available choice beyond those with family wealth.

The test: Which choices become realistically available, to whom, and for how long? Evaluate income stability, unmet needs, and people’s options alongside employment outcomes.

A claim on the gains.

Greater prosperity should reach people who do unpaid care, cannot work, or do not own capital. A society’s claim on the gains can rest on the collective knowledge, public investment, infrastructure, and institutions that help make production possible.

Taxation, broader ownership, public investment, and shared returns offer different ways to distribute prosperity beyond wages. The gains may accrue across firms using AI as well as its developers. A public claim should follow where returns actually land.

Ownership arrangements also raise questions of governance. Who directs investment, who bears losses, and who decides when returns are paid out? A dividend and a democratic voice are distinct benefits; neither should be assumed to deliver the other.

The test: Who receives the gains after taxes and transfers, who accumulates wealth, and what reaches people outside paid employment?

A richer everyday life.

Higher incomes mean more when housing, healthcare, education, and care are accessible. Greater productive capacity should help us provide more of what people need, improve its quality, and reduce the share of household resources required to obtain it.

Cash can expand purchasing power, but shortages and market power can limit what it buys. More supply, public provision, and changes in market rules may be necessary to turn greater income into better living standards. AI’s usefulness will differ across these problems; many require investment and institutional changes beyond technology.

Some of the prosperity we want will take the form of more human attention: smaller classes, more time with a care worker, and public services that can respond to complicated lives. Productivity elsewhere can help support those commitments if we choose to direct resources toward them.

The test: Can people actually obtain better housing, care, education, and services? Track affordability, availability, quality, and the time required to gain access.

A guarantee we can rely on, gains we can share.

Basic security should hold even if the AI boom disappoints. Essential commitments need durable financing from a broad base. Public claims on extraordinary gains can expand what we provide, but speculative abundance cannot finance a dependable guarantee.

Cash, services, and ownership are not interchangeable. Cash gives people discretion. Services can secure access to essentials. Ownership can provide claims on returns and, depending on its design, control. A serious agenda must explain the combination it proposes and how it will be paid for.

There are real choices here. Universal benefits and earnings replacement answer different needs. Stronger support requires resources that have other uses. Expanding purchasing power without expanding constrained services may raise prices. Public investment can generate returns but also carries risk. These choices need explicit distributional and fiscal analysis.

Everyone should be able to count on basic security. Greater prosperity should expand the lives they can choose beyond it.

Working policy ambitions, not estimates of fiscal cost or program effects. Further work should specify benefit adequacy, coverage, delivery, financing, and the distribution of gains, including for people outside paid employment.

A working argument for continued discussion.
