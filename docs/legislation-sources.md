# Legislation sources

Where the bills in the Legislation section (of the Government action tab) come from, how the list is made, and how to refresh it. The data file is
`data/legislation-bills.js`, generated by [`tools/legislation.py`](../tools/legislation.py) from what
[`tools/openstates_fetch.py`](../tools/openstates_fetch.py) and [`tools/congress_fetch.py`](../tools/congress_fetch.py) saved. Do not edit it by hand: the next build overwrites it.

## Where each part comes from

| Part | Source | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| Federal bills | U.S. Government Publishing Office, [Bill Status XML Bulk Data](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS) | Our own list, below: every bill and joint resolution of the 119th Congress whose title names AI or a close relative of it, found by [`tools/congress_fetch.py`](../tools/congress_fetch.py), each with its official record: titles, introduction dates, committees, latest action, stage, and the Congressional Research Service's summary and subject terms where it has written them. U.S. Government work, public domain, no key. |
| State bills | [Open States](https://openstates.org), which follows each legislature's own record | Our own list, below. Public-domain data, attribution requested. Needs a free API key. |
| State summaries (Illinois, New Jersey, Maryland) | Each legislature's own site | For a state bill Open States has no summary for, the legislature's own: Illinois' "Synopsis As Introduced" on the bill's status page, New Jersey's statement at the end of the introduced text, Maryland's synopsis line. Read by [`tools/state_summaries.py`](../tools/state_summaries.py) into [`content/legislation-state-summaries.json`](../content/legislation-state-summaries.json), as the legislature wrote it. |

Nothing is taken from NCSL; see "NCSL" below.

## The state list

It is our own definition, applied to the legislatures' records, and it is deliberately narrow. Three steps, then a checkbox:

1. **Found.** A full-text search of every session since 2025 in every state, DC and Puerto Rico, once for each phrase in `TERMS` in
   [`tools/openstates_fetch.py`](../tools/openstates_fetch.py): "artificial intelligence", "deepfake", "digital replica" and "synthetic media". A bill is
   found because its text says one of them. The build counts a phrase only once its search is finished in every session (`finished_terms` in
   [`tools/legislation.py`](../tools/legislation.py)): a half-finished search finds bills in the states it reached and none in the rest, and the map
   shades each state by how many bills it has, so the page would show how far the search got as if the legislatures had caused it. Where each
   phrase stands is under "Where it stands".
2. **Active.** The page's definition (introduced and not failed). A state bill is active when it became law, or when its session has not ended
   (Open States' session dates) and it has not failed: its latest action is not a failure or withdrawal, and the governor has not vetoed it with the veto left
   standing (a veto counts wherever it sits in the bill's actions, whether it is sustained, pending or tabled: see "Vetoes" below).
3. **About AI, or only mentioning it.** A bill is about AI when its title or the legislature's own summary names artificial intelligence or a close
   relative (AI, machine learning, generative, deepfake, synthetic media, digital replica, artificially or digitally generated or altered content,
   chatbot, large language model, algorithmic, automated decision, automation displacement; the list is `AI_NAMED` in the tool). The search also
   finds a school code or a resolution about Taiwan that says the phrase once. Those bills are kept, flagged `passing` in the data, and shown only
   when the checkbox "Include bills that only lightly mention AI", beside the Status menu, is on, where each is marked "Mentions AI only in its text". With
   it off (the default) they are in no count, no shading and no table, and the note under the table says how many more it would add.

The page says what the list is, and how many bills each rule left out, in the note under the table; the numbers come from `LEGISLATION_FEEDS.openstates`
(`found`, `included`, `passing`, `omitted`).

What a state bill carries: its official title; the legislature's own summary where it gives one (capped at 450 characters, ending in an
ellipsis when cut: Open States' abstract, or, for Illinois, New Jersey and Maryland, which it has none for, the synopsis or statement the legislature
publishes on its own site; California's is the Legislative Counsel's digest, which often opens with "Existing law"); the
legislature's subject index terms as it wrote them (minus any that only repeat the title); its latest action (Open States' own, unless that is dated in the
future, as when Georgia lists a bill's effective date as an action: then the latest action already taken, so that a bill is not sorted to the top by recency); its stage, read from the standard
classification Open States gives each action (passed by a chamber, signed or became law, vetoed, referred to a committee, introduced); and a link to the
bill on the legislature's own site, or on Open States when the legislature has no page for that bill that is not generic.

### Vetoes

A state bill the governor vetoed is failed, and the page does not list it, unless the legislature acted on it again after the veto. This was fixed on 2026-11-24, after a scan of the page's data on 2026-11-23 found California's SB 7 and SB 11 at Passed both chambers with "Veto sustained." as their last action.

**What was wrong.** The stage rule (`os_stage` in [`tools/legislation.py`](../tools/legislation.py)) ended a bill when its LAST action carried one of a few classes, a veto among them, so a veto followed by any action Open States gives no class was lost. California's Senate adds "In Senate. Consideration of Governor's veto pending." after a veto, and "Stricken from file." and "Veto sustained." when the veto stands; New York adds "TABLED" (class `amendment-deferral`). Four bills passed both chambers, were vetoed and stayed listed: California SB 7 and SB 11 (veto sustained on 2026-03-02), California SB 903 (vetoed on 2026-09-30, "Consideration of Governor's veto pending") and New York A 3411 (vetoed on 2026-11-20, then "TABLED", which does not even say veto). Two California bills vetoed the same day as SB 903 (AB 2575 and AB 2656) were never listed, because "Vetoed by Governor." was their last action: the same event was listed or not according to whether the clerk wrote one more line.

**The rule** (`os_veto_stands`, used by `os_stage`). It reads Open States' classification, not its words. A governor's veto of a whole bill is `executive-veto`, and a vote that failed to override one is `veto-override-failure`. The rule finds the last action with either class (the actions in order by date, then by Open States' own order within a day: `os_sorted`). The bill is failed unless a LATER action is classified as a chamber passing the bill (`passage`), an override (`veto-override-passage`), a signature or the bill becoming law (`OS_REVIVES`): then the legislature acted on it again, and the rest of the stage rule places the bill as before (an override or a signature is Enacted, as it always was). A passage before the veto does not count. A bill with no classified veto is placed exactly as it was.

**Words decide nothing, except to leave a veto out.** The text of an action is never used to decide a stage. It is used for one thing: to leave out a veto that is not a rejection of the bill (`OS_VETO_NOT_OUTRIGHT`). A line-item or partial veto is one (Ohio's "line item veto receipt" is classed `executive-veto` by Open States, and the bill is law without the vetoed items; Open States' own class for these, `executive-veto-line-item`, is not read at all), and so is a conditional or amendatory veto (New Jersey's "Conditional Veto, Received in the Assembly": the governor asks for changes and the legislature may agree, which New Jersey's A 3540 did). A bill such a veto leaves is not failed by this rule: it keeps the stage its other actions give it, which is the rule for any bill this rule cannot place.

**A pending veto counts as a veto that stands.** A bill whose veto is still pending (an override is possible until the session ends) could be kept at Passed both chambers. It is not, for three reasons. (1) The page's own definition lists "vetoed" among the ways a bill fails (`LEGISLATION_STAGES.failed`, and the note under the table, "failed, were withdrawn or vetoed"), and the stage rule already treated a veto as final whenever it was the last action (AB 2575 and AB 2656 were never listed): whether a bill is listed should not depend on a clerk's "veto pending" line. (2) It undoes itself: when the source records an override, `veto-override-passage` makes the bill Enacted at the next build. (3) California's session ends formally on 2026-11-30 and New York's on 2026-12-31, after which the session-ended rule drops a bill that did not become law anyway. To keep pending vetoes listed instead, the rule would have to read a veto's text ("pending"), which it avoids; it can be done if the owner wants it.

**The build's net.** The build prints a `veto:` line (`listed_with_a_veto`) when a bill that is listed, and not law, says "veto" in its latest action. That is the check under both the state rule and the federal gap below: a conditional veto is the expected exception, and anything else means a veto the rule missed. It prints nothing when there is none, as now.

**Federal bills.** `stage_of` in [`tools/congress_fetch.py`](../tools/congress_fetch.py) reads no vetoes. None of the 358 federal bills the scan keeps (329 rows) has a veto or a presidential action other than the four that became law (checked on 2026-11-24 against the cached GovInfo files), so nothing is wrongly listed today. A vetoed federal bill would stay where its passages put it until a rule is written for GovInfo's own wording, which cannot be tested without a real example: the `veto:` line would show it. The change of 2026-11-24 touched `tools/legislation.py` only, so the weekly job's copies of the two fetch tools are still current.

**What changed on 2026-11-24.** The rule was compared bill by bill with the old one over all 2,309 state bills the searches found, from the same cached pages: four bills changed, California SB 7, SB 11 and SB 903 and New York A 3411, each from Passed both chambers to failed, and nothing else. Passed both chambers fell from 9 state bills to 5 (the 4 that carry both chambers and their dates, and California's SCR 82 with only its Senate). All four were about AI, so the default list fell from 786 bills to 782, the omitted count rose from 1,407 to 1,411, and with the checkbox on California has 59 bills (it was 62) and New York 216 (it was 217). Tests: `tests/test_legislation.py` (the rule on the real shapes of California's SB 7 and SB 903, New York's A 3411 and New Jersey's A 3540, in any order), and `tests/interactions.js` (the vetoed bills are not in the data, not in a state's Passed both chambers, and a search for "veto" finds only bills that are law).

**Found on the way, not changed** (outside a veto that stands): (a) four appropriations bills look wrongly failed in the other direction. Missouri HB 2002, HB 2005 and HB 2011 ("Vetoed in Part by Governor (G)") and Utah HB 3 ("Governor Line Item Veto") were passed by both chambers and sent to the governor, and a line-item veto applies to a bill the governor has signed, but Open States gives no class to the signing and their sessions have ended, so the session-ended rule fails them. They mention AI only in passing, so they would sit behind the checkbox. (b) New York A 9091 is failed because its last action, "HELD FOR CONSIDERATION IN CONSUMER AFFAIRS AND PROTECTION", is classed `failure`: a committee holding a bill is not the end of it (A 7611, with the same action followed by another, is listed). (c) A single `veto-override-passage`, the override in one chamber, makes a bill Enacted (the rule has always counted it as law, though the second chamber may not have voted yet); no bill is in that position today.

### State summaries

Open States carries a legislature's own abstract for most states. For Illinois, New Jersey and Maryland it carries none, so those bills showed a dash for
a summary and their titles alone said little (Illinois' is "AI-MEANINGFUL HUMAN REVIEW"). Each of those legislatures publishes a summary of each bill on its
own site, and [`tools/state_summaries.py`](../tools/state_summaries.py) reads it, as the legislature wrote it, into
[`content/legislation-state-summaries.json`](../content/legislation-state-summaries.json) (the first 1,200 characters, with where it was read and when; the page
shows the first 450, like every state's summary). Nothing in that file is written by us: a summary made up from a title or two would be a guess, so a state
without a reader keeps its dash. `tools/legislation.py build` uses an entry only for a bill Open States has no abstract for, counts it as the legislature's own
summary when it decides whether a bill is about AI, and has the note under the table say where the summaries come from (`LEGISLATION_FEEDS.openstates.summaries`).
An Illinois synopsis is the one "As Introduced", so a bill amended since may read slightly behind; a New Jersey statement is the introduced version's.

To add a state, write a reader in `tools/state_summaries.py` (a function from a bill row to the text, what it is and where it was read, or `None`) and list it in
`READERS` and `READER_INFO`; it needs a legislature page that gives a summary in its own HTML. Many do not (Pennsylvania, Michigan, Utah and Montana pages load
their text by script or link to PDF analyses), and for those the choice is a dash or a summary we write from the bill's full text, which is a larger job: see
"Limits to know".

### Limits to know

- **Narrower than NCSL's list.** Compared once with NCSL's database (below), on the list for "artificial intelligence" alone: 412 of our 445
  (93%; the list was 445 then) were also on NCSL's active list, but NCSL's has about 1,650. Three causes. The search misses bills that never say "artificial
  intelligence" (only 39% of NCSL's deepfake bills were found, and 27% of its housing bills, for example algorithmic rent pricing); the three
  extra phrases are meant to close the deepfake part of that gap, and how far they do cannot be re-measured, because nothing of NCSL's is kept.
  The "about AI" step puts bills that mention it only in passing behind the checkbox. And the active rule is stricter: NCSL still lists as pending
  many bills from sessions that have plainly ended (Hawaii, Georgia, Virginia).
- **The end of a session is the formal one.** In Massachusetts the formal session ended July 31, 2026, though committees kept acting on bills into
  October, so none of its bills shows as active; North Carolina and South Carolina are similar. In California the session's end date in Open States
  is November 30, though the last day to pass bills was in August, so its unpassed bills show as active until then.
- **Territories:** Open States covers Puerto Rico and none of the others, so Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana
  Islands have no bills here. Each state is as complete and as current as Open States' scraper for it; the `latest_bill_update` it reports per jurisdiction says how fresh.
- **To widen the list**, add a phrase to `TERMS` in `tools/openstates_fetch.py` (or pass `--term` for one run). Each phrase costs about 108
  requests, one per session, and the build ignores it until it is finished. The "about AI" step still applies.

## Federal bills

The list is our own, found by [`tools/congress_fetch.py`](../tools/congress_fetch.py) in GovInfo's own data. No third party's list is used or compared with (the
owner replaced an earlier, curated list with this one on 2026-11-16). The tool downloads GovInfo's bill-status zips for the 119th Congress
(bills, `hr` and `s`, and joint resolutions, `hjres` and `sjres`: about 46 MB, refreshed several times a day while Congress sits; kept in `~/.cache/ai-tracker/congress`
and fetched again only when GovInfo's copy has changed), reads every bill in them (17,174 on 2026-11-16) and keeps the ones that match the rule below. Simple and
concurrent resolutions (H.Res., S.Res., H.Con.Res., S.Con.Res.) are not read: they state opinions and never become law.

- **The rule** (the owner's, 2026-11-16: AI named outright plus its close relatives, with two relatives narrowed after a first scan found them mostly matching something
  else). Strict terms: artificial intelligence, AI or A.I. (in capitals), superintelligence, machine learning, large language model. Close relatives: generative, chatbot,
  deepfake, digital replica, automated decision, frontier model or frontier AI, autonomous weapons or lethal autonomous, and algorithm, algorithms or algorithmic when a
  *title* says it. "Autonomous" alone had matched the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region and autonomous-vehicle bills; "algorithm" in a summary had matched grocery and Medicare
  price bills.
- **Two tiers.** A bill is listed when any of its titles (short, display or official, as introduced, reported or passed) has a term. One that only the Congressional Research
  Service's summary names (48 of 351 on 2026-11-16) is written with `passing: true` and hidden until the checkbox is on, as a state bill that only mentions AI in its text is.
  A bill that says AI only deep in its text, beyond its title and its summary, is not found; nor is a new bill the CRS has not summarised yet whose title does not say it.
- **Companions.** GovInfo marks a House bill and its Senate companion as "Identical bill"; they share one row (`H.R. 8031; S. 4216`), with the more advanced stage, the later
  last action and both introduction dates.
- **What a row carries.** The display title; the stage, read from the actions ("Passed/agreed to in House", "Became Public Law"; any referral to a committee is "in
  committee"); the status words (introduction dates, committees, whether it became law); the latest action; the CRS summary where one exists (87 of 351 on 2026-11-16: the
  latest version, with the title that opens it dropped, capped at 450 characters), else the official title when it is not already the display title (12 rows have neither,
  because their title is the official one); the CRS's subject terms where it has assigned them (90 of 351; it indexes new bills late); and a link to the bill's page on
  Congress.gov (GovInfo gives the address for most bills; for the rest it is built from the bill's kind and number).
- **What was tried and does not work.** The CRS subject terms as a way to find bills: it indexes new bills late (90 of the 351 matches carry any) and none carries
  "artificial intelligence". The Congress.gov API as the source: it has no full-text search, so finding AI bills would take tens of thousands of calls at 5,000 an hour, where
  GovInfo's zips hold the same records in one download.
- **Where it is thin.** Titles are most of what finds a bill, so a bill whose title is a bare acronym and whose summary the CRS has not written is missed (a text pass over the
  Congress.gov API or GovInfo's bill texts could add them, slowly).

## NCSL

NCSL's [Terms of Use](https://www.ncsl.org/terms-of-use) (updated May 5, 2017) say NCSL owns the material on its website and that you may not
"reproduce, duplicate or copy material from ncsl.org without permission", nor republish or redistribute it. Attribution does not stand in for
permission. The first version of the state list came from NCSL's AI Legislation Database; on 2026-11-12 the owner asked for NCSL's summaries too,
was told what the terms say, chose to leave them out, and then asked for our own list built from official records instead. So nothing of
NCSL's is in the data any more: not its list, its summaries, its subject headings or its topic tags. Linking to its database as a reference is
fine. The terms do not mention automated access. A one-off comparison against an export of NCSL's database (nothing kept from it) produced the
numbers under "Limits to know". Whether looking bills up in official records is enough on its own is a judgment, and this is not legal advice;
asking NCSL for permission remains the safe route to use anything of theirs.

what is loaded. From the top, in every section: the toolbar, the topic chips (always showing, above the search), the search with Sort and the result line, then the map. The Reset button (the owner, 2026-11-18: the small Clear pill and the Clear filters link were hard to see, and she chose one Reset button) is at the right end of the sticky toolbar and is always there, the same size as the Sort menu: solid, with a count of what is on, while anything differs from how the tab opens, and an
outline otherwise. It puts the whole tab back as it opens: no jurisdiction, no filter in any section (the bills' search, status, topics and checkbox, and the lists' search and chips, including those of a section that is not showing), the order back to Most recent, and the Legislation view (where the tab opens; it was All until 2026-11-24); the count
is the jurisdiction, the search words, the status, each topic, the checkbox, the order if it is not Most recent, and the section if it is not Legislation. Pressing it with nothing to reset says so. The toolbar, the topic chips and the search bar are all sticky, in that order, each under the one before, as the filter bars on Papers and the Fact Bank
are (the owner asked on 2026-11-18 for the topics to be part of the sticky bar in every section, and for the note under them, which said what a topic is and how many bills have none, to go: the rule for assigning a topic

### The bills table's columns, the order of progress, the chambers and dates, Federal and the CSV export (2026-11-23)

The owner's reviewers' feedback of 2026-11-23, built the same day (their last item, linking the policy cards to the legislation, is round 2 and is not built; see the end of this section).

**Sorting by a column.** The Status and Last action headers of the bills table are buttons (inside the `th`, which says `aria-sort`; the arrow beside the word is SVG, so the header's text is unchanged). There is ONE sort state, `legView.sort`: the Sort menu in the search bar and the two headers both set it through `legSetSort`, and the headers show it, so they cannot disagree. The menu has five choices, Most recent action (the default), Oldest action, Status least progress first, Status most progress first and Jurisdiction; a first press on Last action gives the newest first, a first press on Status gives the least progress first, and a press on the column that is already the order reverses it. Jurisdiction belongs to no sortable column, so both headers say `none` under it. The Enforcement and Cases lists have no Status or Last action column (a row has a date, who acted, the matter, an outcome and a source), so they keep their three orders; under a Status order their Sort menu shows Most recent, which is how they are listed (the one setting is kept for when the bills come back, and Reset puts it back).

**Searching them.** There is one search box, and it matches everything these two columns show: the stage ("enacted"), the chamber or chambers and the dates a bill passed ("passed house", "march 2 2026"), and the last action's date, written both ways it can be asked for ("2026-05-10" and "May 10, 2026") and its words ("held at the desk"). Every word typed must be somewhere in what the row shows, as before. The Status menu is the filter for the stage. No second search was added: a per-column box would say the same thing twice.

**The order of progress.** The Status column sorts by how far along a stage is, never alphabetically (alphabetical would put Enacted before In committee and before Passed). The ranks are `rank` in each entry of `LEGISLATION_STAGES` in `data/legislation-data.js`, which is the one place the order is kept (the Status menu and the stats bar read it too): Introduced 1, In committee 2, Passed one chamber 3, Passed both chambers 4, Enacted 5. A bill that failed (died in committee, was vetoed or withdrawn, or its session ended) has rank 0, below every stage that is alive; failed bills are never listed (only active bills are), so the rank is only what keeps the rule whole should a failed stage ever be shown. A stage with no rank sorts after all the others, either way. Within a stage the bill acted on most recently comes first, in both directions. Every stage the data has today was ranked: introduced, committee, one-chamber, both-chambers and enacted (the real bills are 2% introduced, 77% in committee, 4% passed one chamber, 1% passed both chambers and 16% enacted). A test pins the ranks and fails if the page sorts by anything else.

**Which chambers passed it, and when.** A bill at the one-chamber or both-chambers stage carries `passed`, a list of `{chamber, date}`, one entry for each chamber that passed it, in the order they passed it, and the Status cell says "Passed House, September 30, 2026" under the stage, or, for a bill that passed both, "Passed Senate, May 19, 2026; Assembly, August 30, 2026" (the owner, 2026-11-23: "Specify which chambers in the government action tab"; the first version, built earlier the same day, named the chamber for the bills that passed one chamber only). The data is made from the bills' own actions, with no new request to any source: for a federal bill `passages_of` in `tools/congress_fetch.py` (the Library of Congress's "Passed/agreed to in House" or a chamber's own "Passed Senate ...", the same lines the stage is worked out from, saved in `candidates.json`), and for a state bill `os_passages` in `tools/legislation.py` (Open States' standard classification `passage`, by the upper or lower chamber, from the pages already in the cache, which hold every action). The chamber is the source's own word: House or Senate federally, and for a state the legislature's own name for the chamber, so California, New York, New Jersey, Nevada and Wisconsin say Assembly and never House. A chamber that passed a bill more than once (New York re-passes bills in the second year of its session) gives its latest date, the vote that stands; a federal bill and its identical companion share the row, so a chamber that passed either counts. The two are listed in the order they passed it (the earlier date first; the House, or a state's lower chamber, first when it was the same day): that order is decided once, in `merge_passages` (federal) and `os_passages` (state), and the page, the search and the CSV show the list as written. Nothing is guessed: a bill whose source gives no chamber name or no parseable date gets no `passed`, and its Status cell says "Which chamber passed it, and when, is not in our data." ("Which chambers passed it, and when, is not in our data." at the both-chambers stage); a both-chambers bill with only one of the two in its data shows that one, then "The other chamber and its date are not in our data." On 2026-11-23 all 21 federal and all 77 state bills that passed one chamber carried it, and 8 of the 9 state bills that passed both carried both chambers (4 of the 5 after the veto fix of 2026-11-24; the scan has no federal bill at the both-chambers stage: the four federal bills that passed both are law, and the code is there for the first that is not); the build prints how many do, and which do not, every time (`passed:` lines). Only a bill at those two stages has one: an enacted bill passed both chambers too and the scan has its dates, but the chambers are named for the two stages that are about them, and the tests check that no other stage carries it. **A committee's vote is not a chamber's passage.** Open States classifies some committee actions as a passage too, and one bill is affected: California's SCR 82 is at the both-chambers stage because its two Assembly "passages" are committee votes ("From committee: Be adopted and re-refer to Com. on APPR."). `os_passages` leaves out an action that is also classed as a committee's vote (`OS_COMMITTEE_VOTE`), so the Assembly is not named for it: its Status cell reads "Passed both chambers" over "Passed Senate, July 10, 2025. The other chamber and its date are not in our data." The stage itself is `os_stage`'s, which counts those committee actions, and was left alone (SCR 82 is probably at Passed one chamber, and whether the stage rule should change for it is the owner's call; California's SB 7 and SB 11, which were at Passed both chambers with "Veto sustained." as their last action, were fixed on 2026-11-24: see "Vetoes" above).

Columns, in order: Type, Jurisdiction, Bill number, Session, Title, Status, Chambers that passed it, Dates it passed, Last action date, Last action, Status note from the source (a federal bill's "Introduced ... Committee: ..."), Who acted, Date of the action or ruling, Outcome, Checked on, Summary (a bill's; for a case or action our own description), Topics (ours), Mentions AI only in its text, Source (a bill's source, or the agency's or court's host) and Link. Dates are YYYY-MM-DD, which a spreadsheet reads and sorts. It is written in the page and saved with a Blob and a temporary link (`legExportCsv`), so it needs no server and works from `file://`; the file is `ai-bills-<place>-<YYYY-MM-DD>.csv` (`all`, `federal`, or the state's name with hyphens, such as `new-york`; the date is the reader's own today), or `ai-government-action-<place>-<YYYY-MM-DD>.csv` when it also holds the cases and actions. Every cell is quoted, a quote inside is doubled, and a line break inside stays inside its quotes. A cell that starts with `=`, `+`, `-` or `@` (or a tab or return) gets a leading apostrophe, so a spreadsheet shows it as text and never runs it as a formula (the usual CSV-injection guard; it changes how such a cell reads, by one character, and none of the real bills has one). Lines end CRLF and the file starts with a byte order mark (EF BB BF) so Excel reads the typographic quotes and dashes in titles and summaries. *Inside an iframe:* the deployed page is embedded in the owner's site, and a browser can block a download started in a frame. What is known: Chrome and Edge refuse a download from a frame whose `sandbox` attribute does not list `allow-downloads` (a plain `<iframe src=...>` with no `sandbox` attribute, which is what an ordinary embed is, is not affected), and they also refuse one started in a cross-origin frame with no click behind it, which the Export pill has (it is a button the reader presses). What was observed (2026-11-23, real Chrome, a test page that put the dashboard in an iframe with `sandbox="allow-scripts allow-same-origin"` and no `allow-downloads`): a real mouse press on Export made the page report "Exported 786 bills to ai-bills-all-2026-11-23.csv." and no file was saved to the Downloads folder, which is what a block looks like from outside; the page cannot tell the difference (a browser gives a page no signal that a download was refused), so its message is the same either way. The contrast (the same press in a frame that is allowed to download) was not run on that machine, because it would have written a file into the owner's Downloads folder; the same button works in a normal tab in the jsdom tests (they read the Blob it saves). So: if the embed on the site has a `sandbox` attribute, add `allow-downloads` to it, or the pill will appear to do nothing there; if the web person's setup also sends a `Content-Security-Policy: sandbox` header for the page, that needs `allow-downloads` too; and opening the dashboard in its own tab always works.

## Refreshing

```sh
python3 tools/openstates_fetch.py                       # every phrase in TERMS, saved to ~/.cache/ai-tracker/openstates; resumes where it stopped
python3 tools/openstates_fetch.py --refresh-open        # a weekly refresh: fetch again the sessions still in progress (12 on 2026-11-17, about 100 requests)
python3 tools/state_summaries.py                        # the legislatures' own summaries for the bills Open States has none for (about 8 minutes the first time)
python3 tools/congress_fetch.py                        # the federal bills: downloads what changed at GovInfo (about 46 MB the first time), scans it in a few seconds; no key
python3 tools/legislation.py build
```

then reload the page, open the Legislation tab and run `node tests/interactions.js`.

**The weekly job runs COPIES of two of these tools** (`~/.local/share/ai-tracker-refresh/`, installed 2026-11-17: see the README there). `tools/congress_fetch.py` now also saves which chamber passed each bill and when (`passed`, 2026-11-23), so after this change copy it again, or the job keeps writing a `candidates.json` without it, and the next build gives every federal bill that passed one chamber the line "Which chamber passed it, and when, is not in our data.":

```sh
cp tools/openstates_fetch.py tools/congress_fetch.py ~/.local/share/ai-tracker-refresh/
```

(`tools/openstates_fetch.py` did not change: the state passage is worked out in `tools/legislation.py` from actions the cache already holds.) The build says so when it happens (`passed:  N of M federal bills ... without: ...`). `build` also takes `--states-cache <folder>`,
`--federal-cache <folder>` and `--dry-run` (print what it would write); `congress_fetch.py` takes `--refresh` (download again) and `--no-download`.

- **The key.** Open States' API needs a free key (sign up at https://openstates.org, generate it on your profile). Save it, one line and nothing
  else, in `~/.openstates_key` (mode 600), or set `OPENSTATES_API_KEY`. It stays out of the repository, the cache and every log. The free key allows
  10 requests a minute and 250 a day; the fetch spaces requests 7 seconds apart, waits a minute if it is limited anyway, stops with a message when
  the day's allowance is spent, and saves every page as it arrives, so an interrupted or stopped run carries on and a finished one costs nothing to
  repeat. The whole list is about 500 requests (the 175 for "artificial intelligence" and about 330 for the other three phrases), so a first run
  takes two or three days of the free allowance, and so does a re-fetch from an empty cache (delete the folder to re-fetch). A saved page never gets newer by itself (a run that finds a page saved costs nothing), so the state bills are only as
  recent as the day they were fetched. `--refresh-open` (built 2026-11-17) fetches every page again for the sessions still in progress (no end
  date, or one that has not passed): about 100 requests while a dozen legislatures are in session, more in the spring, and a session that has ended
  is not fetched again. It replaces a session's pages only once all of them have arrived, and `--max-age` (default 1 day) lets a stopped run carry on.
  The date the page gives for when the state bills were read is the day the oldest saved page of a session still in progress was fetched (`read_day`
  in `tools/legislation.py`), not the day the build ran, so refreshing moves it forward. Asking Open States for a higher limit is the other way to
  shorten the first fetch.
- **The weekly refresh job (installed on the owner's Mac, 2026-11-17).** A launchd job runs every Monday at 7:30 AM (at wake, if the Mac was asleep), only
  while the owner is logged in, and refreshes the cache: `openstates_fetch.py --refresh-open`, then `congress_fetch.py`. It writes only to
  `~/.cache/ai-tracker` and to its log, `~/Library/Logs/ai-tracker-refresh.log`; it never touches the repository; and it tells the owner by a macOS
  notification when it is done or when a step failed twice. After that, run `python3 tools/state_summaries.py` and `python3 tools/legislation.py build`
  by hand (about two minutes) and commit `data/legislation-bills.js` and `content/legislation-state-summaries.json`. The job lives in
  `~/.local/share/ai-tracker-refresh/` (outside Documents, which macOS can keep a background job out of), with a README.txt of its own. The `.py` files
  there are COPIES of `tools/openstates_fetch.py` and `tools/congress_fetch.py`, so when either one changes (a new search phrase in `TERMS`, say), copy it
  again: `cp tools/openstates_fetch.py tools/congress_fetch.py ~/.local/share/ai-tracker-refresh/`. Run it now with
  `launchctl kickstart gui/$(id -u)/local.ai-tracker.legislation-refresh`; remove it with
  `launchctl bootout gui/$(id -u)/local.ai-tracker.legislation-refresh` and by deleting
  `~/Library/LaunchAgents/local.ai-tracker.legislation-refresh.plist` and that folder. It replaces the scheduled Claude task
  `finish-openstates-search`, which switches itself off once its search is finished.
- **What stops the build:** an unknown jurisdiction or session, a date that does not parse, a bad link. It writes nothing if it stops.

## Enforcement actions and key cases

The Enforcement and Cases sections of the Government action tab list what agencies, attorneys general and courts have done about AI (Enforcement the actions of an agency or an attorney general, Cases what a court has decided or is deciding), from `data/legislation-actions.js` (`LEGISLATION_ACTIONS`). The Google Sheet is the master: the `enforcement` and `cases` tabs of the same spreadsheet as the research list (the columns, and how to fill a row in, are in [`content/README.md`](../content/README.md)). The daily sync (`tools/actions.py`, the second step of `.github/workflows/research-sync.yml`) is the only writer of the file and of its snapshots `content/enforcement.csv` and `content/cases.csv`, and it publishes only the rows whose `status` is `approved`, so a row can be written over several days, and read by someone with legal training, before it goes live. The file's own header holds the row format the page reads. The rules are strict: only the agency's or the court's own page is a source (never a news story, a law firm's note or a tracker's write-up); every entry is checked against that page before it goes in and read by someone with legal training; the words are ours, and an allegation is "alleged", never "found" (most actions are settlements without an admission); an outcome carries the date it was checked (`checked` in the Sheet, `asOf` in the file), because a case moves. Nothing here is legal advice and the page says nothing like it.

The first batch went in on 2026-11-17: 14 rows (13 enforcement actions and one case), each checked that day against the page its `url` points to (an agency's release or case page, a state attorney general's release, or a court order on govinfo.gov), with every date taken from that page. It has NOT been read by someone with legal training, which the rule above requires before an entry is live. On 2026-11-18 the 14 rows moved into the Sheet as `approved`, which keeps the page as it was; each row's `notes` says the read is pending, and setting a row to `under review` takes it off the site at the next sync. An outcome is where the page leaves it on the day in `checked`, not a docket check. Two are the first to settle (their `notes` say so): the FTC's own case pages still say Pending for Evolv and for Rite Aid, and for Evolv the signed order is dated December 5, 2024. The page's old separate status word (Pending, Final) is folded into the outcome ("Lawsuit filed (pending)"). Found but not in yet, because each needs its own agency or court page read first: the state attorneys general's suits against OpenAI after Florida's (eight states, October 2026), and further SEC "AI washing" and FTC cases. A pasted brief said Texas and Oregon had sued OpenAI; no page we found says so, so those are not in.

How it shows. Until the file has a row of a kind (until a row of that kind is approved in the Sheet), that kind's section is a placeholder; the first row turns its list on. Actions and cases are two sections with their own lists and counts, and are never counted with the bills or with each other in a count, a title or the masthead (the one exception is the shading of the map on All, which adds the three up as items and says so in its legend). A list shows ten rows to a page, with the Papers pager under it, as the bills' table does (the owner, 2026-11-18: a max of ten a page). The sections are by KIND (All, Cases, Enforcement and Legislation are one tab, switched under its masthead; All holds everything), and the place is shared: the Show menu is the Legislation menu (Federal there is Congress; here it is the federal agencies and courts), the place chosen in any section stays chosen in the others, and the address carries it (`#enforcement/texas`, `#cases/texas`). A row's place comes from its `jurisdiction` in the Sheet (Federal, or a state, DC or Puerto Rico), which the tool turns into `level` and `state` in the file: a federal row is listed under Federal, a state attorney general's action or a state court's case under its state. The map's card on All counts the rows of the place it shows in a block for each kind (Enforcement and Cases show only their own block), with no titles and, wherever the place has rows, a "See more details below" link to that list (`enfCardBlock` in `src/js/55-enforcement.js`); a place with none says "None listed", never "none", since the list is chosen by hand and is not complete. A row's topics (the `topics` column, the labels of `LEGISLATION_TOPICS` separated by semicolons) are the same list the bills' topics come from, so a topic means the same on both tabs; they are picked by hand, because the word rule that finds the bills' topics matched only three of the first 14 rows. The topic chips show only the topics some row bears on. To add a row: check the agency's or court's page, add a row to the Sheet's `enforcement` or `cases` tab with that page as its `source` (leave `status` blank while it is being written and read), set `status` to `approved`, and the next sync lists it (Actions tab > Run workflow does it at once). Nothing is edited in the repository. The About page's Government action paragraph says what the tab lists (`src/views/50-about.html`); change it if the tab's scope changes.

## On the page

`data/legislation-bills.js` is fetched the first time any of the four sections opens (`legLoadBills` in `src/js/60-legislation.js`, through
`loadPayload` in `src/js/05-lazy.js`: the card in all of them counts from it, and the map on Legislation and All is shaded from it), so the other tabs never wait for it. It adds its rows to `LEGISLATION_DATA` and sets
`LEGISLATION_FEEDS`, which the page reads for the sources line under the map and for the note under the table.

That line follows the section. On Legislation it cites the sources of the bills (GovInfo and Open States, once their file has loaded); on All also our own list of key cases and enforcement actions; on Enforcement and Cases our own list alone. Where it cites dated sources it ends with "Last updated" and a date: the newest of the sources' dates, which
is the day each feed was read (`read` in `LEGISLATION_FEEDS`). Each source's own date stays in its
citation just before it, so a feed that is a day older than the other still says so. A source with no date adds none, and a line with no dated source says
nothing about when it was updated. What moves the date: a rebuild after the weekly refresh moves the feeds' dates.
